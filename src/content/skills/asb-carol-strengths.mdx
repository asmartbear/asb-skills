## What this is about

A pile of honest observations is not yet a strategy input. It is
evidence that still needs a verdict. This skill runs two steps that
turn it into one. The first step is **distill**. Many observations
often point to one deeper truth. For example, "customers post
screenshots of support wait times" becomes the attribute "slow tech
support." A smaller set of strong attributes is easier to remember and
act on. Each candidate attribute must pass the Opposite Test. If no
successful company would ever claim the opposite ("we love our
customers"), the attribute is too generic. It is not strategy. The
skill does not record it.

The second step is **classify**. Here the book solves a problem: almost
any attribute can be argued both ways. Whether something is a strength
or a weakness depends on who you ask. So the skill uses a clear rubric
to decide. An attribute is a strength if more than a third of the
market sees it that way, or if some customers buy for it alone. An
attribute is a weakness if more than a third of the market sees it that
way, or if it makes the product impossible for some customers, who
refuse for this reason alone. If both are true, the attribute is
*both*. The skill records it twice and flags it, because a
both-attribute marks a place where market segments disagree. A clear
strategic choice is waiting there. If neither is true, nobody cares.
The skill drops the attribute, but keeps a record of the decision, so
you stop spending attention on it. The result is the exact chart that
your keystones, deal-breakers, and finally your ideal customer come
from.

## Example invocation

You can invoke the skill like this:

```text
/asb-carol-strengths Here's our OBSERVATIONS.md from the honest-look
exercise — 19 observations. Distill it down and tell me what we're
actually working with.
```

The skill proposes attributes one at a time. Each attribute cites the observations behind it. Each attribute is tested with the Opposite Test. You and the skill classify each attribute against the rubric. The skill records the results in `STRENGTHS-WEAKNESSES.md` as you go.

One practical note: say where you are working. Name a directory, or
point to your existing files, when you invoke the skill. The skill then
keeps the method's files together in that location. If you do not say,
the skill asks before it creates anything.

## From the source

Two sources form the foundation of this skill:

- Chapter 2 of *Hidden Multipliers*: *Carol Loves You for You* (sections "Distill Into Important Attributes" and "Classify Strengths and Weaknesses") — [hiddenmultipliers.com](https://hiddenmultipliers.com). This chapter gives the merge-or-drop distillation method, the genericness trap, and the two-question classification rubric that this skill uses.
- [**The "Opposite Test"**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/opposite-test/) — this is the genericness gate. A claim means something only if a successful company could reasonably claim the opposite.

Supporting sources each supply a piece of the mechanism:

- [**Selling to Carol: Why targeting an ICP brings 10x more customers than you expected**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/icp-ideal-customer-persona/) — this shows where these classified attributes lead: the trade-offs that Carol loves you for, and that everyone else weighs.
- [**"It's a Balance" isn't always the answer**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/conflicting-choices/) — this explains why a both-classified attribute forces a choice. It is not a compromise. When market segments disagree, making a clear choice is the core of strategy.
- [**Specificity: A weapon of mass effectiveness**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/specificity/) — this sets the wording standard for attributes. Each attribute must be specific enough to create the edge that separates your ideal customer from everyone else.
