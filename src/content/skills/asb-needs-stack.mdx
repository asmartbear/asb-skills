## What this is about

No one wants to buy your product for its own sake. The customer who
creates an AWS account does not want infrastructure. They want the
WordPress website it enables. They do not really want the website
either. They want a personal platform for content and self-promotion.
They want that platform because they are chasing a book deal. They
want the book deal because of a goal above it. There is always
another level. This ladder is your customer's **Needs Stack**. The
book's key insight is this: a product that satisfies one level makes
every product below it *obsolete* for that customer. Whoever delivers
the website without the infrastructure wins the customer. The
infrastructure vendor never even sees them.

Mapping your stack answers questions that your competitor list cannot
answer. It shows who your real *alternatives* are: vendors one level
above you who quietly take customers out of your market. It shows
what outcome you can honestly promise. That outcome is the level just
above yours — your features are what you do, and the next level up is
what you promise. It shows which steps you should brag about making
obsolete. It shows which metric tells you whether customers actually
succeed. And it shows where your company's higher purpose lives.

This skill builds the map by asking you questions, one level per
exchange. First it anchors the level your product satisfies. State
this level as your customer's goal, in your customer's own words, not
your product category. Then the skill walks down through the steps
you make unnecessary. It also walks up through what the customer
really wants. Every level must pass a check before the walk moves to
the next level: exact wording, a true means-to-an-end link, and real
named occupants. Do not sketch the whole ladder in one step. Do not
stop at your own level — the chapter warns that this is a common
mistake.

## Example invocation

You can invoke the skill like this:

```text
/asb-needs-stack We sell managed Postgres hosting for small SaaS teams.
Here's our CAROL.md from the ideal-customer exercise — what does our
customer actually want, and who are our real alternatives?
```

The skill anchors your level in your customer's words. Then it walks
the stack down and up with you. It records each finished level in
`NEEDS-STACK.md`, with its occupants and its positioning role.

One practical note: tell the skill where you are working. Name a
directory, or point to your existing files, when you invoke the
skill. The skill then keeps the method's files together in that
location. If you do not name a location, the skill asks before it
creates anything.

## From the source

Two sources form the foundation of this skill:

- As in Chapter 4 of *Hidden Multipliers*: *A Rose by Any Other Name* (section "The Needs Stack") — [hiddenmultipliers.com](https://hiddenmultipliers.com). This section explains the stack itself. It covers the rule that a level makes the levels below it unnecessary. It covers the trade-off between the shortest path and flexibility. It covers the level-role framework this skill records: features at your level, promises one level up, aspirations above, and advancements below. It also covers the purpose levels at the top.
- [**Using the Needs Stack for competitive strategy**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/needs-stack/) — this article gives a deep, published treatment of the topic. It covers alternatives versus competitors. It covers Google searches as a proxy for the level your customer is thinking at. It covers how to measure the next level up. It also covers why climbing your own stack is usually a different business.

Supporting articles each supply a piece of the mechanism:

- [**How repositioning a product allows you to 8x its price**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/more-value-not-save-money/) — why the stack matters for your business: the level you position at sets the value you can charge for.
- [**Selling to Carol: Why targeting an ICP brings 10x more customers than you expected**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/icp-ideal-customer-persona/) — this shows whose needs you must map: build the stack for your ideal customer. The stack differs for anyone else.
