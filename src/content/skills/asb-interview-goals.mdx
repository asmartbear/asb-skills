## What this is about

You cannot ask a customer "What should we charge?" The customer will only
shrug. You cannot ask "Would you buy this feature?" The customer will give
a polite "yes." That "yes" disappears when the invoice arrives. But
pricing, positioning, and product decisions are exactly what customer
interviews must answer. Jason's interview method solves this problem. He
refined it over twenty years. Its first step is also the step most people
skip: write down your **goal questions**. These are the questions you need
answered but cannot ask directly. Number them G1, G2, and so on. Every
hypothesis and interview question you write later must trace back to one
goal question.

This skill interviews you about your business. Your business can be a new
idea or a company twenty years old. It can be B2B or B2C. The skill then
works with you to build a goal-question list for your situation. It does
not copy anyone's template. When the list is sharp, the skill writes
GOALS.md. GOALS.md holds your context in prose, then the numbered goal
questions. [The interview workshop](/workshops/customer-interviews/) uses
this file in its later steps.

## Example invocation

You can invoke the skill like this:

```text
/asb-interview-goals We run a four-year-old B2B SaaS for property managers,
about $40k MRR, mostly small firms. Growth stalled this year. We're debating
a price increase and whether to move upmarket, and honestly we've never done
real customer interviews.
```

The skill interviews you about the decisions you need to resolve. It turns vague goals into questions your interviews can actually answer. Then it writes GOALS.md: your context in prose, then numbered goal questions G1, G2, and so on.

The skill also works when you have no customers and no product yet, just an idea to validate:

```text
/asb-interview-goals My startup idea is an AI-powered phone system for
plumbers, to schedule appointments or diagnose simple issues while the
plumber is busy. Solo operators. Validating before I build anything.
```

This goal list looks completely different. The decision here is "build it at all?" So the goals target pain, cost, and coping behavior, not pricing or positioning.

One practical note: tell the skill where you are working. Name a
directory, or point to your existing files, when you invoke the skill.
The skill then keeps the method's files together in that location. If
you do not name a location, the skill asks before it creates anything.

## From the source

This skill is based on two sources:

- As in Chapter 10 of *Hidden Multipliers*: *A multiplier of one's own* (section "How to Extract Insights from Customers") — [hiddenmultipliers.com](https://hiddenmultipliers.com) — describes the five-step interview method. This skill performs its first step: goal questions. The chapter gives the standard eleven-goal list for a typical B2B company. It also tells the WP Engine story, which shows what the method achieved.
- [**The Iterative-Hypothesis customer development method**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/customer-development/) — the published article that describes the same method: goals, then hypotheses, then open-ended questions, then iterate, then stop when it gets boring.

These sources give words and reasons for specific goal questions:

- [**Selling to Carol: Why targeting an ICP brings 10x more customers than you expected**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/icp-ideal-customer-persona/) — defines Carol, the ideal-customer persona. Several standard goal questions are about Carol.
- As in Chapter 3 of *Hidden Multipliers*: *Speaking Carol Into Existence* — [hiddenmultipliers.com](https://hiddenmultipliers.com) — covers keystones, deal-breakers, inciting events, and identifying attributes. The "What does Carol look like?" group of goals is designed to find these.
- [**Using the Needs Stack for competitive strategy**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/needs-stack/) — describes the higher-level outcomes a customer expects as a byproduct. The final standard goal question targets this.
- [**Yes, but who said they'd actually BUY the damn thing?**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/customer-validation/) — explains why you need real customer evidence, and why "I'm my own customer" is a trap. This is why this exercise exists.
- [**When you have nothing: How to find potential customers to interview**](https://longform.asmartbear.com/find-customers-to-interview/) — tells you where to find interviewees once your goals, and later your questions, are ready.
